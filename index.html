AI Statecraft · Technology Governance · International Political Economy

I study AI governance as statecraft. Rather than treating AI governance only as a matter of technology ethics or domestic industry regulation, I examine how states use compute, data, algorithms, technical standards and alliance networks as instruments of power: to compete with rivals, to shape international order and to secure themselves. My current work focuses on the hardware layer of this contest: US semiconductor export controls, China's countermeasures, and the Southeast Asian economies through which both are routed.

Research Agenda

AI Statecraft: governing AI as an instrument of state power

Much of the debate on AI governance frames it as a problem of ethics, safety and domestic regulation. I approach it instead through the lens of tech and digital statecraft. Export controls on chips, screening of compute clusters, rules on cross-border data, restrictions on open-weight models, safety evaluations and standard-setting are not only technical choices. They are the means by which states mobilise resources, build coalitions, deter rivals and defend national security inside the international system.

Focal question

Core puzzle

The paradox of securitisation and open innovation

  • Research question. When states turn AI governance into a security instrument, by restricting open-source models, screening compute clusters or limiting the mobility of researchers, how far do these measures actually constrain competitors, and how far do they backfire on the home country's own innovation ecosystem and the vitality of its open-source communities?
  • Theoretical dialogue. Realism, which puts relative gains first, against liberalism, which stresses absolute gains and technology spillovers.
    Realism relative gains first

    Denial pays if it widens the gap with the rival. Costs borne at home are the price of preserving relative advantage.

    vs.
    Liberalism absolute gains and spillovers

    Denial forfeits scale, talent and spillovers, shelters the rival's substitutes and erodes the openness on which frontier innovation depends.

Evidence so far, from semiconductors. My working projects treat chip export controls as the first test of this paradox:
Durable in law, porous in trade. Controls backed by allies survive in law but leak through Southeast Asian connector economies, while unilateral ones are bargained away once their costs land at home or the target answers back (Project 01).
Controls shelter the rival. A US control acts as an import ban Beijing did not have to impose, enriching Chinese makers of substitutes (Project 02).
Revocable access drives exit. Once access looked conditional, Chinese buyers walked away even after the licence was restored (Project 03).
Blocked trade reroutes. US exports of controlled chips and tools to China fell after the 2022 rule, while exports to Southeast Asia doubled after the 2023 update (Project 04).
Coercion spends itself. Each use of a chokepoint finances the alternatives that erode it (Project 05).

Work in Progress

Working Projects

  1. Fences in a Common Market: Withdrawal Resistance and the Durability of US Chip Export Controls, 2022–2026
    Research paper 2026 Comparative case study

    Why do some US chip controls endure while others are reversed within months, under the same statute and against the same target? The paper introduces withdrawal resistance: leakage through connector economies, home-cost feedback and counter-dependence, moderated by allied lock-in. It shows that legal durability and economic decoupling are distinct outcomes, and that Southeast Asian connector states now decide how much US technology controls bite.

    Full text (PDF)
  2. Borrowed Protection: Who Pays for US Export Controls on Advanced Computing?
    Research proposal 2026 Firm-level incidence

    For the Chinese market, a US export control works as an import ban that Beijing did not have to impose. Using financial statements on both sides (Nvidia, Applied Materials, Lam Research, SMIC, Naura, Cambricon), the proposal explains who bears the cost of a control, when, and in which line of the accounts, and why relaxing a control does not bring a lost market back.

    Full text (PDF)
  3. The H20 Licence: Restriction, Reversal, and Refusal, April–September 2025
    Case study 2026 Firm lobbying · host-state power

    Nvidia lobbied a single-product licence requirement away, at the price of 15 per cent of its China revenue, only for Chinese regulators to steer domestic buyers off the chip. The case asks why the firms with the strongest interest in a restricted input stop buying it, without contest, at the moment it becomes legally available again.

    Full text (PDF)
  4. Chip Controls and the Direction of US Exports
    Data report 2026 Trade panel · diversion

    A 61,128-observation product–destination–month panel of US exports built from USITC DataWeb, 2019–2026. Exports of targeted products to China fell 39 per cent in the year after the October 2022 rule, while exports to Southeast Asia doubled only after the 2023 update. The dataset is designed for a difference-in-differences test of whether the controls redirected trade rather than ending it.

    Full text (PDF) Code & data (GitHub)
  5. Overplaying the Rare-Earth Card: Why Reliability Is China's Best Leverage
    Policy commentary 2026 Southeast Asia · critical minerals

    Every use of the rare-earth weapon finances the alternatives that erode it, from Japan after 2010 to Malaysia's emerging processing hub today. The commentary argues that predictable supply is Beijing's most durable leverage, and sets out recommendations for China and for ASEAN governments.
